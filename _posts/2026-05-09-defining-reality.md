After years of doing DS-ML-(AI? Somehow I have to add that too now), starting in 2018 with Andrew Ng's classic Coursera course that still ran on Octave/MATLAB, surviving the COVID-19 hackathon trends, ML competitions, projects, graduate school, and now in 2026, my stance has barely budged:

**People with strong domain knowledge consistently seem to thrive at applying new tech, Machine Learning, AI, and whatever comes next, to their own use cases.**

And I suspect this will remain true for quite a long time, although **the boundary will keep moving.**

| Era                              | Technology gets better at                                  | Domain expert still provides                      |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| **Classical ML & Deep Learning** | Feature extraction, representation learning, prediction    | **Problem formulation + data understanding**      |
| **LLMs & AI Agents**             | Increasingly autonomous reasoning, planning, and execution | **Defining reality, objectives, and constraints** |
| **Future Tech??**                | ???                                                        | ???                                               |

### Moving Up the Stack

Classical machine learning required a lot of manual work. Someone had to design features, clean the data, tune the model, and eventually turn the notebook into something useful and production-worthy solution.

Deep learning removed some of that burden through representation learning. We still do plenty of feature engineering, data analysis, debugging, and all the usual messy work around the model.

Now LLMs and AI agents are starting to absorb another layer. They can write code, search for information, analyze data, plan steps, and execute increasingly complicated workflows.

So, yeah, human contribution keeps moving upward.

In many projects I've worked on, knowing the algorithm was rarely the hardest part. Usually its around: *What exactly are we trying to predict? Is this data actually meaningful? Is this strange value an error or a real phenomenon? What would make the result useful to the people who actually do the work?*. The better the technology gets at the lower layers, the more these questions matter.



### But there's a catch

I wouldn't bet that domain experts will always win.

A person can know a domain deeply and still fall behind if they ignore what new technology makes possible. Someone with enough domain understanding and strong AI literacy can move much faster.

So the best move may come from **domain expertise + AI literacy**.

That does not require knowing every model architecture or becoming an AI researcher. It means understanding the technology well enough to know where it works, where it fails, how to apply it to a real problem, and how much credit you can honestly take for the output.

> **AI reduces the cost of intelligence, but it doesn't eliminate the value of knowing what intelligence should be applied to.**

### So, when does it finally stop mattering?

Maybe when AI can independently understand our data, our instructions, and the messy reality behind them.

A tad bit higher bar: When it can tell that a business rule is outdated. When it notices that a sensor is lying. When it recognizes that the objective itself was poorly defined.

At that point, domain expertise may matter much less because the AI would already understand the context that domain experts normally provide. We would be asking it to understand **reality itself**. And even then, somebody still has to decide what outcome is worth pursuing.

### What can we do to stay relevant?

Probably by continuing to move up the stack.

That territory keeps changing, whether we like it or not.

Learn your domain deeply enough to recognize what actually matters. Learn the technology well enough to understand what has suddenly become cheap and easy. Then spend more of your attention on framing the problem, checking assumptions, setting constraints, making trade-offs, and taking responsibility for the result. All while still not assuming whatever AI generates automatically becomes your own (that is usually how we end up producing slop).

Maybe the future advantage comes from being the person who can tell an increasingly capable AI:

**This is the problem worth solving. This is how. This is what good looks like. And this is what must never go wrong.**

Be **Reviewer #2**.

The good one, though.
